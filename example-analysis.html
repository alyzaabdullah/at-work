SYNC: example analysis

How I would check whether SYNC's measures are consistent, shown end to end. See the same simulated people in the example therapist dashboard.

Simulated dataEvery number on this page comes from made-up data I generated to test the analysis pipeline. It says nothing about how SYNC performs with real people. No children or real participants are involved.

Question

If the same person plays SYNC twice, a week apart, do they get similar scores? A measure has to be consistent before it can be used to track change. As a second check: does SYNC detect a difference everyone expects, between a person's dominant and non-dominant hand?

Data

12simulated adults
8 weeksone session a week; this analysis uses days 1 and 8
1,440trials on days 1 and 8 (30 per hand, per day)
117,493raw tilt samples at 60 Hz, days 1 and 8

The simulated data has the same structure SYNC records: one row per trial (hit, endpoint error, reaction time, movement time, timing against the beat) and the raw tilt trace for every trial.

Method

  • Smoothness: for each trial, the tilt trace is low-pass filtered at 8 Hz, then scored with the log dimensionless jerk (LDLJ; Balasubramanian et al., 2015). Values closer to zero mean smoother movement.
  • Per person: each measure is averaged over a person's 30 trials for each hand on each day.
  • Consistency: test-retest reliability between day 1 and day 8 (dominant hand), as an intraclass correlation, ICC(2,1), absolute agreement. Interpreted with Koo & Li (2016): below 0.5 poor, 0.5–0.75 moderate, 0.75–0.9 good, above 0.9 excellent.
  • Expected difference: dominant versus non-dominant hand on day 1, paired t-test with effect size (Cohen's dz).

Results

In this simulated data, smoothness has good day-to-day consistency (ICC 0.76). Beat timing variability is the least consistent measure (ICC 0.33), so on its own it would be a poor choice for tracking progress.

Limitations

  • The data is simulated, so these results only show that the pipeline runs and produces sensible output.
  • Twelve people is a small sample; real reliability estimates need more.
  • Adults, not children. Children's movement and attention differ.
  • No comparison yet with a standardized test (such as the MABC-2 or BOT-2). That validation is the next step.
